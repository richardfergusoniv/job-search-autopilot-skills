# Rejection Review

## Purpose

Every rejection is either noise or signal. This skill separates the two: reconstruct what was submitted against what the posting actually needed, classify where in the funnel it died, and produce reframing guidance, how to position the user's real experience so the next application in that role family covers the shortfall. The goal is better framing, never fewer applications. It never invents a reason the employer did not give.

## Workflow

1. **Gather evidence, no guessing.**
   - Tracker record: company, role, submitted date, pay band, notes.
   - Materials sent: the resume and cover letter in the role's application folder. If the cover letter is unavailable (some workflows purge letters after submission), note that instead of reconstructing it from memory.
   - Posting requirements: the pipeline state entry, notes from around the submission date, or a re-fetch of the official posting if it is still live.
   - The rejection itself: full email text and response date. Compute the time from submission to rejection.

2. **Classify the funnel stage.**
   - *Screen stage:* rejected within about 72 hours of submission, and/or a pure form email. The cause lives in the resume, the ATS parse, or a hard gate (title, years, location, pay band).
   - *Later stage:* survived more than a week, or the email references conversations or interviews. The cause lives in fit, pay expectations, or competition.
   - Record whether the email states a specific reason (rare) or is form language (usual).

3. **Diagnose against the pre-submission gates.**
   - Was a core-requirement mismatch flagged before applying? Say plainly when a flag was raised and overridden. The review judges decision quality, not just outcome.
   - Title or seniority gap? Pay band against the user's floor? Location or work-mode friction?
   - *Role-family mismatch:* same vocabulary, different profession (the classic case: two fields that share words like "portfolio," "operations," or "reporting" but mean different work). This is the highest-value diagnosis; it looks like a fit to keyword matching but not to a human screener.
   - *Materials framing:* was the resume or letter angled at the wrong family? Is the most relevant experience buried? Does the summary line mean something different to this screener than intended?

4. **Build the reframing playbook.** Maintain `references/reframing-playbooks.md`. For the rejected role's family, write or update the entry: what this family actually screens for, which of the user's real experiences maps to it, and how to lead with that mapping (summary line, bullet order, cover letter angle). The playbook is the durable output; the next application in that family uses it. Two or more screen-stage rejections in one family mean the playbook entry needs rework, not that the family gets dropped. A drop is proposed only when a gap is genuinely unbridgeable with honest reframing, and only with the user's explicit confirmation.

5. **Log it.** Append one dated row per rejection to the rejection log: date, company, role, stage, diagnosis, reframe.

6. **Report to the user:** one short paragraph. What the role actually needed, where the profile missed, and the concrete change. Lead with the diagnosis, never with tracker mechanics.

## Output contract

Every review ends with: (a) a stage classification (screen / later), (b) a primary-cause hypothesis, labeled as hypothesis when the email is a form rejection, (c) reframing guidance: the specific resume and cover letter changes for the next application in that family, (d) a log entry.

## Operating rules

- Never contact the employer to ask for feedback on a rejection.
- Never fabricate a reason. Form rejections get "no reason given"; diagnose from timing and posting fit only.
- One rejection is a data point, not a verdict. Repeated rejections in a family mean the playbook entry needs rework, never a unilateral decision to stop applying there.
- **Reframe, don't remove.** A gap between the posting and the user's background is a positioning problem first. Find the honest bridge: which real experience maps to what they screen for, and lead with it. Name the translation explicitly in the summary and cover letter instead of hoping the screener makes it.
- Don't rewrite history: if a gate flag was raised and overridden before submission, say so plainly.
- Keep it short. The user gets the diagnosis and the adjustment, not the methodology.
