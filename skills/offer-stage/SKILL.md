# Offer Stage

## Purpose

An offer is where the numbers stop being a posting and start being a contract. This skill separates the three jobs that get tangled at that point: the arithmetic (what the offer is actually worth), the paper (what the documents actually say), and the conversation (what to ask for, in the user's voice). It surfaces tradeoffs and open questions. It never recommends signing or declining, and it never interprets contract language as legal advice; interpretations become questions for a lawyer.

## Workflow

1. **Gather the documents: work from the paper, not the phone call.**
   - The full offer letter text, plus any equity plan summary, grant notice, or benefits sheet that came with it. Note plainly which documents were mentioned but not provided.
   - The advertised range from the original posting (tracker record or the saved posting in the role's application folder).
   - The user's anchors from their profile: desired base (their standing floor), current base if they want a raise figure, and anything they have said about stage or location preference.
   - The deadline the employer stated, if any. An exploding deadline is a fact to surface early, not at the end.

2. **Run the salary-gap analysis.** From the letter's numbers, compute and report: year-1 total comp, steady-state total comp (after any sign-on or one-time items fall away), the base gap against the user's desired base in dollars and percent, where the offer sits inside the advertised range, and the flags: below-floor base, equity unpriced. Equity with no dollar value is never guessed at: totals exclude it, and the missing numbers (strike price, current fair market value or 409A, percent of the company, vesting schedule) become explicit asks in step 4.

3. **Walk the contract.** Go clause by clause against the actual documents. The checklist: base, bonus (target, basis, payout timing), equity (type, amount, vesting, cliff, exercise window), sign-on (and any clawback), start date, title and level, work location and remote terms, benefits and PTO, probation or at-will terms, arbitration (and any jury-trial or class-action waiver inside it), confidentiality, non-solicit, non-compete, prior-inventions and IP assignment scope, outside-work restrictions, severance and termination terms, and any repayment obligations. For each clause mark one of: present and standard, present and unusual, absent. Everything unusual, or absent and material, goes onto the **lawyer question list**: questions only, phrased for a lawyer, with the clause quoted so the lawyer does not have to hunt for it. Two items always surface at the top, never buried:
   - Any non-compete or non-solicit. If the user currently has no restrictive agreement, signing one is a change in their legal position, not a detail.
   - The prior-inventions and IP scope, checked against the user's existing side projects and any work they do outside the job.

4. **Draft negotiation talking points.** Anchored on the step-2 analysis: the range position, the base gap, and the priced (or unpriced) equity. Talking points only, in the user's voice: direct, no bluffing, no ultimatums, no fabricated leverage. Where a gap cannot close on base, cover the honest bridges: sign-on, equity clarity, review timing, title. A competing process may be mentioned only when it is real.

5. **Log it.** Append one dated row per offer to the offer log: date, company, role, offer base, steady-state total comp, flags, open questions.

6. **Report to the user.** Lead with the numbers and the flags, then the lawyer question list, then the draft talking points. End with what is still unknown. Never end with a recommendation.

## Output contract

Every offer review ends with: (a) the analysis breakdown: year-1 total comp, steady-state total comp, base gap versus desired (dollars and percent), advertised-range position, and the flags; (b) the clause walk: present, unusual, or absent per clause, with the lawyer question list as its own section; (c) negotiation talking points anchored on the numbers; (d) an explicit list of what is unknown or unpriced (missing documents, unpriced equity, undefined terms); (e) no recommendation to sign or decline, in any wording.

## Operating rules

- **Not legal advice.** Contract language produces questions for a lawyer. Never state what a clause means for the user legally, whether it is enforceable, or whether a term is "fine." "Typical in the market" is context, not a conclusion.
- **Never advise signing or declining.** Surface the tradeoffs, the gaps, and the open questions. The decision is the user's, including the decision not to negotiate at all.
- **Below-floor base is a flag, not a verdict.** The user's profile states their floor and whether an early-stage upside exception applies. The analysis flags it; the report frames it; nobody auto-decides it.
- **Equity is not comp until it is priced.** Shares without a strike, a current valuation, and a percent-of-company figure are recorded as unpriced, with totals shown without them. Never annualize a guess.
- **Never fabricate leverage.** No invented competing offers, no invented deadlines, no number the user would not actually accept.
- **Nothing goes to the employer without the user's approval.** Drafts are drafts. No email, portal reply, or script gets used until the user approves the final text.
- **The user's current legal position is the baseline.** If they have no non-compete or restrictive agreement today, and their IP includes their own side projects, any document that changes either fact is a headline item, not a footnote.
- Keep it short. The user gets the numbers, the questions, and the draft lines, not the methodology.
