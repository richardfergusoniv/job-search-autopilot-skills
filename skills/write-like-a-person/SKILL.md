# Write Like a Person

## Purpose

Every surface of a job search is writing: application free-text answers, recruiter emails, cover letters, thank-you notes, LinkedIn messages. Recruiters read hundreds of these, and text that sounds machine-generated quietly costs the user interviews. This skill is the standing voice pass for everything the system writes on the user's behalf. It rewrites AI-sounding text so it reads like the user wrote it, without changing what it says and without inventing anything.

Apply it to drafts produced by the other skills before they ship, and whenever the user pastes text and asks for a cleanup.

## Why AI text sounds the way it does

A language model picks the wording that fits the widest range of readers and subjects. A person writes for one reader and one subject, so their choices are uneven and specific. Every pattern below is a form of the default choice. The fix is always the same direction: more specific, less staged.

## How to work

1. **Mark the tells.** Read the whole text once and mark every pattern below, strongest first. Look at paragraph shape as well as sentences; a contrast split across two sentences or the same closer after every paragraph is the same tell at a larger scale.
2. **Rewrite.** Keep every supported claim. Shorten dull parts, merge or split sentences, change structure, but keep the information. Never add a fact, name, number, or date that is not in the source or the user's fact sheet. If a sentence needs a detail you do not have, write a simpler sentence.
3. **Check the draft.** Read it aloud. Ask what still sounds generated, and whether the rewrite added or dropped any fact. Then search again for the tells that survive rewrites most often: contrasts, closers, triads, dashes, bold labels.
4. **Match the user's voice.** If the user has provided a writing sample, it overrides these patterns, including the dash rule: match its sentence length, word choice, and punctuation habits. Without a sample, take the voice from the kind of text: a recruiter email is short and direct, a cover letter can carry one opinion or aside, a form answer is plain.

## The patterns

### Staging instead of stating

- **Not X but Y.** "It is not just about efficiency, it is about trust." The negative half names something nobody claimed, so the positive half sounds larger without saying more. State the point directly. Keep a contrast only when the negative half corrects something the reader actually believes.
- **One-line closers.** A short paragraph that restates the one before it ("That is the real difference."), or a sentence after an example that names what it showed ("This shows my commitment to..."). Cut closers that repeat. A short sentence earns its place only when it adds a fact.
- **Sayings that sound deep.** "At its core," "the real question is," "what really matters," "fundamentally." Replace the saying with the specific claim.
- **Staged run-ups.** "I am writing to express my strong interest," "I would love to take this opportunity to," "Let me introduce myself." Remove the announcement and make the point.
- **Arguing with no one.** "I am not saying I am perfect, but..." or rejecting an option nobody raised. Remove the defense; state the claim it was protecting.

### Rhythm by rule

- **Forced triads.** Ideas arriving in threes to sound complete ("passionate, driven, and detail-oriented"). Check each item adds something distinct. Keep three real items; merge or cut decorative ones.
- **Dashes as the universal connector.** The final text contains no em dashes unless the user's own writing uses them. Replace with a period, comma, colon, or parentheses. A dash lets the writer skip deciding how two clauses relate, so models reach for it constantly.
- **Stacked qualifiers.** "I could potentially possibly contribute..." Keep a qualifier only when the meaning needs it.
- **Passive voice hiding the actor.** "Results were achieved." In application writing the user is the actor; name them. "I built," "I cut," "I led."

### Inflation and borrowed authority

- **Overused AI words.** Delve, leverage (for ordinary use), pivotal, crucial, robust, showcase, underscore, tapestry, testament, meticulous, vibrant, intricate, landscape (abstract). One is a habit; a cluster is a tell. Use the plain word.
- **Inflated significance.** "A pivotal moment in my career," "played a key role in the company's transformation." Keep the fact, drop the significance. End on the last concrete fact, not a send-off.
- **Sales language.** Passionate, thrilled, excited to apply, dream company, groundbreaking. State what the user did and why this role, specifically.
- **Borrowed authority.** "Recognized by leadership as a top performer" with no source. If the fact sheet supports it, state the concrete version (the award, the promotion, the number). If not, cut it.
- **Avoiding is and has.** "Serves as a senior associate" is "is a senior associate." Use the simple verb.

### Formatting by rule

- **Bold as decoration.** Bolded phrases scattered through a letter or email, or lists where every item gets a bold label. Remove it. Bold is for the one thing the reader must not miss, which in job search writing is almost nothing.
- **Chatbot residue.** "I hope this email finds you well," "Great question," "Please do not hesitate to reach out," "Looking forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience." Cut the wrapper, keep the content. A plain "Thanks for your time" is human; a paragraph of courtesy formulas is not.

### Wrong reader

- **Re-explaining what the reader knows.** A reply to a recruiter that restates the whole search before answering their question. In a thread, the reader has the context. Lead with the answer; keep only the fact they lack.

## Job search specifics

- Generic claims ("hard worker," "team player," "fast learner") are the application version of sales language. Each one either gets a concrete fact to carry it or gets cut.
- A sentence that could be pasted into any other application is a defect. Every sentence should contain something specific to this user, this company, or this role.
- Vary sentence length. Short sentences are fine. The user's real emails are usually shorter than a model's default; when in doubt, cut.

## When not to act

A person can use any one of these patterns on purpose. Leave a phrase alone inside a quotation, a title, or the user's own unedited words. Salutations and sign-offs predate chatbots. Several tells together are the signal; one watched word is not proof of anything.
